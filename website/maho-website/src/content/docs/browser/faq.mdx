# Frequently Asked Questions

These answers cover the most common questions people ask when they compare Maho with Arc, Nook, and Zen. The short version is that Maho is built for people who want a modern, privacy-first browser with native apps on macOS, iOS, and Android, plus a workspace model, built-in AI, and encrypted sync.

## What makes Maho different from other browsers?

Maho combines a Rust core engine with native UI on each platform, so it does not rely on an Electron shell or a single web app interface. That gives it a different feel from many modern browsers, both in performance and in how closely it follows platform conventions.

The product is also workspace-first. Spaces, Boosts, Split View, Notes, Reading List, Easel, Profiles, and built-in AI are part of the browser, not add-ons you need to assemble yourself. The goal is to make the browser feel organized and personal without forcing a heavy setup process.

Privacy is another major difference. Maho does not ship telemetry by default, supports end-to-end encrypted sync, and offers a local-first AI option for people who want to keep model requests on their own machine. That combination is still uncommon, especially on mobile.

## Is Maho available on Windows or Linux?

Not yet. Maho is currently available on macOS, iOS, and Android. Windows and Linux are on the roadmap, but there is no public release timeline to share today.

If you need Linux support right now, Zen is the closest of the browsers covered here. It is Firefox-based, cross-platform, and available on macOS, Windows, and Linux. The tradeoff is that Zen does not currently offer iOS or Android apps, so it solves a different part of the market.

For now, Maho is focused on delivering a polished experience on the platforms it already supports rather than stretching the same product across every desktop and mobile system at once.

## How does Maho compare to Arc Browser?

Arc pioneered the workspace browser category, and a lot of the ideas people now expect from this space were popularized there. Maho shares some of that DNA. It has Spaces, Boosts, Split View, and Easel, so people coming from Arc will recognize the overall workflow quickly.

The biggest difference is momentum. Arc is still downloadable for Mac and Windows, but The Browser Company has pivoted its attention to Dia, so Arc is no longer being actively developed in the way it once was. Maho, by contrast, is an actively maintained product.

Maho also fills in gaps Arc never fully addressed. It includes iOS and Android apps, end-to-end encrypted sync, and built-in AI tools with BYOK and local model support. If you liked Arc's style of browsing but want something that is still moving forward, Maho is a more current option.

## Can I use Chrome extensions in Maho?

Yes, Maho supports Manifest V3 extensions. The current limitation is that extensions must come from unpacked directories, so there is no Chrome Web Store integration yet.

In practice, most MV3 extensions work with little or no modification, which makes it possible to bring over a lot of the browser tooling people already use. That said, extension compatibility is always shaped by the extension itself, so a few tools may need adjustment.

The comparison with other browsers is straightforward. Arc inherited Chromium extension support, Zen uses Firefox Add-ons instead of Chrome extensions, and Nook does not support extensions at all. Maho sits somewhere in the middle: it supports the Chromium extension model, but with a narrower install path for now.

## How does Maho handle privacy?

Maho is designed to keep privacy defaults strong without making the product hard to use. It does not send telemetry by default, and its sync system is end-to-end encrypted so the relay server only stores encrypted data it cannot read.

The browser also includes a built-in content blocker, per-site permissions, and profiles for separating different browsing contexts. For AI, you can choose local-first providers such as Ollama, which keeps model activity on your own machine, or use your own OpenAI or Anthropic key if you prefer cloud models.

Nook takes a similarly privacy-first approach, though it stays much simpler and does not include sync, AI, or extension support. Zen inherits Firefox's tracking protection and has a strong privacy reputation as well. Arc was less centered on privacy as a product theme than either Maho or Nook.

## What AI features does Maho include?

Maho includes a set of built-in AI features aimed at everyday browsing tasks. These include Tidy Tab Titles, Tidy Downloads, Page Preview, and Tidy Tabs, plus slash commands such as /summarize, /translate, /explain, /rewrite, /fact-check, and /code-review.

You can bring your own OpenAI or Anthropic key, or use local models on your own hardware. That means the browser can work with cloud AI or stay local depending on your preferences. When you use a local provider, content does not need to leave your device for those requests.

Arc introduced its own AI features through Max, but Maho takes a more integrated approach around the browser workflow itself. Zen and Nook do not ship with built-in AI features in the same way.

## Is my data synced through Maho's servers?

Maho uses a relay server at wss://sync.maho.app, but the data sent through it is end-to-end encrypted. The server stores encrypted blobs and cannot read your browsing data.

That design matters because it lets Maho offer cross-device sync without turning the sync service into a place where your data is exposed in plain text. Offline changes are queued locally and synced once you reconnect, which keeps the browser usable even when the network is unstable.

You can also use Maho without sync at all. If you prefer a fully local setup, that remains an option. Compared with Nook, which avoids accounts entirely, Maho trades a little complexity for cross-device continuity. Compared with Zen, Maho's sync model is more tightly integrated into a mobile-capable browser family.

## How do Maho Boosts compare to Zen Mods?

Both features let you customize websites with CSS and JavaScript, so the core idea is similar. The differences are in how they are packaged and shared.

Maho Boosts include built-in UI for color and font overrides, 10 preset templates such as Dark Mode and Hide Ads, URL pattern matching with glob and regex support, and scope controls that let you apply a boost to a specific space. That makes Boosts feel like a first-class browser feature rather than just a scripting layer.

Zen Mods are more community-driven and are typically installed from a marketplace. That gives Zen a different rhythm, with more emphasis on shared mods and Firefox's extension ecosystem. Neither approach is universally better, they simply serve different users and habits.

## Can I import my data from another browser?

Yes. Maho can import bookmarks, history, and passwords from Chrome, Firefox, Arc, Brave, and Edge. The import wizard runs on first launch, and you can also open it later from settings.

That makes switching much easier, especially if you are coming from Arc and want to preserve your existing browser organization. It also lowers the friction for people moving from Chrome or Firefox, since those are the most common starting points.

Safari import is not supported yet, so Mac users who depend on Safari may need a different migration path for now. Even so, the supported import list covers most of the browsers Maho is likely to replace.

## Is Maho open source?

No. Maho is not open source. Its core is proprietary Rust code, with native platform shells for macOS, iOS, and Android.

That puts it in a different category from Nook and Zen. Nook is open source and free, while Zen is open source under MPL-2.0. Those projects may be better fits if source transparency or community modification is your top priority.

Maho instead focuses on a tightly controlled product experience, cross-platform native apps, privacy-first defaults, and a feature set that is built and maintained as one coherent browser. For some people, that tradeoff is worth it. For others, an open source browser will still be the better choice.
